When it earns its place

Five sessions on making the picture properly. Tonight is whether to make it at all, and the evidence points in two directions at once. A field experiment on a real ad network found fully AI-created ads clicked about nineteen percent better than expert human ones, and the same experiment found that disclosing the AI cut clicks by about thirty-one and a half percent. A survey of two and a half thousand people found that adding an AI watermark did not reassure anyone; it eroded trust further. Both results are real. Read together they say something specific about which assets are worth making this way, and the last thing you will build is the rule for saying no.

Part 1 · covers the click evidence

The ledger that says yes

5 min live

Two studies, both large, both on live advertising rather than in a lab. They are the strongest case anyone has for generated visuals, and each one carries its own qualification in the same paper.

LiveWhat the yes ledger actually supports3 min▶

The NYU and Emory field experiment ran banner ads across the Google Display Network and found fully AI-created ads achieved about nineteen percent higher click-through than ads made by human experts. That is a real result on real traffic, and it is the strongest number in this session. The same experiment found that AI-modified human designs failed to show improvements and sometimes performed worse, which is a strange and useful detail: the premium did not come from adding a model to a human process, it came from ads that were made entirely that way.

The Taboola study is bigger and more cautious. Across more than five hundred million impressions and three million clicks, AI ads took 0.76 percent click-through against 0.65 percent for human ads in the raw data, but the release states plainly that they "performed comparably when researchers applied the tightest statistical controls." And its most interesting finding is not about AI at all: the ads that did the best of any group were AI-generated ads that did not look AI-generated, significantly outperforming both human-made ads and AI ads that read as artificial.

Read on its own, this ledger says generated visuals can match or beat human ones on the metric advertisers care about. It does not say anything about what happens when people can tell.

Self-studyHow far to trust these two numbers3 min▶

Both are advertising studies, which means the outcome is a click, not a purchase, a brand impression or a reader's opinion of a report. An illustration in an annual review or on a product surface is not a banner ad, and nothing in either study speaks to it. Both are also recent enough that nobody has replicated them; the field-experiment numbers reached this page through the authors' own research summary and a write-up rather than the working paper itself, which sat behind a block, so the nineteen percent is a well-corroborated figure rather than one read from the source PDF.

The honest handling is the one session 1 used on vendor documentation: take the number, name where it came from, and do not put it in a headline without the qualification that shares its paragraph. Anyone quoting the nineteen percent without the thirty-one and a half from the very same experiment is quoting half a result.

Part 2 · covers the trust evidence

The ledger that says no

6 min live

Four studies, three of them surveys and one the other half of the experiment above. Their agreement is unusually clean, and one of their findings is genuinely counterintuitive: the transparency measure that is supposed to fix this makes it worse.

LiveThe two ledgers are one result4 min▶

The plus nineteen and the minus thirty-one and a half come from the same experiment, which is what makes this a finding rather than a disagreement between studies. The measured performance premium is conditional on non-detection. The measured trust penalty is conditional on detection or disclosure. They are two readings of one mechanism, and which one you get is decided by what the audience knows.

The TBWA and Ideally survey adds the part that removes the easy escape. Hybrid execution, a human finishing a generated image, reduces the trust penalty but does not remove it. And adding an AI watermark, the thing a responsible team reaches for, "actively erodes trust further, turning transparency into a measurable liability rather than a neutral safeguard." The Nuremberg survey found the same direction: identical ads labelled as AI-generated were rated more negatively, particularly on emotional appeal.

So a strategy that banks on the premium is a strategy with an expiry date, because disclosure is moving toward default and because one in two people in that survey already suspected AI in human-made work. What survives is the Taboola finding, arriving from the pro-AI side of the ledger: the work that wins is work that does not read as AI. Not because it is hiding, but because it was genuinely art-directed and finished by a person. That is an argument for craft and against volume, and it is the argument the five previous sessions have been building.

Self-studyWhere each number is soft, named honestly4 min▶
  • The Getty survey sells the answer it found. Nearly ninety percent wanting disclosure and ninety-eight percent calling authenticity pivotal come from a company whose business is licensing human-made imagery and which is litigating against a model provider. The fieldwork also ran from July 2022 to September 2023, which makes it the oldest data here by a wide margin, in a subject that changes yearly.
  • The TBWA and Ideally work is industry research, not a peer-reviewed study, with about 2,500 respondents across about 3,500 evaluations. Its watermark finding is the one that most deserves replication and has not had it.
  • The Nuremberg survey is representative and small in scope: a thousand respondents in each of three countries, plus one labelling experiment in Germany. Its most transferable finding is a moderator rather than a headline: AI-generated ads were more accepted for innovative, high-technology products and less accepted for traditional ones.
  • Several widely circulated figures are not on this page at all. Numbers from marketing-platform blogs claiming particular percentages of consumers trusting brands less, or particular click-through gains, reached this build as single snippets from vendor-published sources with no methodology attached. They are excluded rather than repeated, which is also why you will not see a figure here for how accurately anyone detects AI images.
Real world

Daybreak's founder asked whether the launch email should say the hero image was made with AI. The honest answer from this evidence is that a label will probably cost engagement, that not labelling may be a choice the law removes, and that the third option is the one their pipeline already supports: an illustration a person art-directed, refused lettering on, composed, typeset and signed off, which is both less likely to read as synthetic and more defensible when somebody asks.

Part 3 · covers the rule

A rule for saying no

4 min live

Two axes decide it: what the artefact is, and how trust-sensitive the category is. Neither axis is about whether the tool is capable; both are about what the picture is doing for the reader.

LiveThree questions, in order3 min▶

One: what is the picture claiming? Decorative claims nothing; explanatory claims that the world works this way; evidential claims that this happened or this exists. Evidential is a no everywhere, because a generated image of a real thing is a false statement regardless of how it was labelled, and the trust penalty is the least of what follows.

Two: how much does this category live on trust? The Nuremberg survey found generated ads more accepted for innovative, high-technology products and less for traditional ones; the TBWA work found high-trust sectors such as airlines and banking hit hardest. A coffee subscription sits in the middle. A hospital does not.

Three: will it read as AI? This is the craft question and it is the one you control. The Taboola result says ads that did not look AI-made beat everything, including human work. Everything in sessions 1 to 5, a locked kit, a composed frame, refused lettering and hand-set type, is how a picture stops reading as generated. A yes on the grid is conditional on that pipeline, not on the tool.

Self-studyWhat to write down, and who signs it3 min▶

The rule is worth four lines on one page, because in practice it is applied by people who were not in this session. Name the artefact classes you make and put each in one of the three verdicts. Name the two or three categories your organisation works in and mark their sensitivity. State the pipeline conditions that a yes depends on, in the words of the control sheet: dated version, named kit, reserved zone, hand-set type, named comparer. And state who decides when the grid says hybrid, by role, because "hybrid only" without a named person is a yes with extra words.

Then the disclosure question, which this page deliberately does not answer for you: what the law requires, where, and by when is owned by learn-ai-media-with-phoebe session 6, and it moves. The evidence here tells you what disclosure costs; the other course tells you when you have no choice. A rule that names both, and says which one wins when they conflict, is a rule that survives a legal review.

Build-along

The rule, then the six assets

★ 22 min · everyone writes

Twelve minutes on the one-page rule for your own organisation, then ten on the final scorecard, scored against the asset set you have carried since session 1.

List your artefacts. The five or six kinds of picture your organisation actually makes: campaign hero, product shot, explanatory diagram, event photo, editorial illustration, social tile. Put each in decorative, explanatory or evidential.

Mark the categories. The sectors you work in, and how much each lives on trust. If you serve a regulated one, it gets its own line and its own verdict, not an average.

State the conditions. A yes depends on the pipeline: dated version, named kit at a recorded weight, reserved type zone, hand-set type, named comparer. Copy those five words from the control sheet so the rule and the sheet cannot drift apart.

Name the decider. By role, for every cell that says hybrid, and for anything the grid does not cover. An unnamed decider is how "hybrid only" becomes "yes" three months later.

Write one no. One artefact class you will not generate, with the sentence that says what it claims. The no is what makes the yeses credible to a sceptical colleague.

Write the rule for the person who joins next year, not for the people in the room. They will have a newer model and the same three questions.

Put both numbers in it, the plus nineteen and the minus thirty-one and a half, with the note that they came from one experiment. A rule with only one of them will be argued with, correctly.

Do not write model names into the rule. Three defaults in fifteen months; the rule outlives all of them, and the control sheet is where versions live.

Add the watermark finding explicitly, because somebody will propose a label as the safe option, and the measured effect of that label is worse trust, not neutral trust.

Point at the two owning courses by name for the legal and disclosure questions, rather than summarising law that changes.

Date it and put a review month on it. Every number in this session has a shelf life, and the Andersen trial reported as starting in September 2026 could change the whole risk narrative.

The hardest cell is decorative and high-sensitivity. A hospital's newsletter header is decorative, claims nothing and sits in the category with the largest measured penalty. The grid says hybrid: generate, art-direct hard, finish by hand, and have a named person sign that it does not read as synthetic. If that feels like more work than drawing it, that is the grid telling you something.

The control sheet names a model version with the date it was checked, and the ratio, seed and references are recorded with their stated ceilings From session 1. Five layers, and the prompt is the fourth of them.
The style exists as six sentences a stranger could act on, including the one about what is absent, and the kit is named files with one job each From session 2. A moodboard is everything you like; a kit is the smallest set that reproduces the sentences.
Every asset has its ratio, its subject placed, and a third of the frame reserved and empty before anything was generated From session 3. Placement lines in words a painter could be wrong about.
No brief asks the model to spell anything, every brief carries the refusal and the zone, and every character of type is set by hand From session 4. The encoder never saw a letter; eleven characters were enough to fail.
A named person compares each run against the six sentences, and the version rule says what happens when the default changes From session 5. Consistency is a pipeline with a person as its last step.
Tonight: the rule exists on one page, names at least one no, and every yes is conditional on the pipeline rather than on the tool From session 6. The premium is conditional on non-detection; the penalty on detection. Craft is what survives both.
Homework

After the course

25 min
  1. Take the rule to the person who would have to enforce it and watch which line they argue with. That line is either wrong or not yet written for them.
  2. Re-check the volatile facts before you teach any of this onward: the default model version, the two terms-of-service dates, and where the litigation stands. All of them moved inside the last year.
  3. Run one asset end to end on the full pipeline and time it against your old loop. Most people find the first is slower and the fourth is faster, because the kit stops being rebuilt every time.
  4. Find the asset you brought tonight, the one you could not classify, and ask the person who made it. Whatever the answer, it is a fact about how much of this is perception.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A colleague cites the nineteen percent click premium for fully AI-created ads. What is the complete reply?

Both numbers come from one field experiment on the same network. Quoting either alone misrepresents it. The same paper also found AI-modified human designs were no better and sometimes worse, which is why the premium is specifically about fully generated work and specifically about undetected work.

2 · A team proposes adding an AI watermark to every generated image as the responsible option. What does the evidence say about that?

The synthetic authorship work found transparency behaving as a measurable liability in its data, which is counterintuitive and unreplicated, and worth stating as what one survey found. It does not follow that you should not disclose: the legal duty is separate, and that is why the rule names both and says which wins.

3 · Which of these is a no on the grid regardless of category or model quality?

The evidential row is the one nobody argues about. A better model makes the false statement more convincing, not more acceptable. Regulated sectors are a hybrid-or-no depending on the row, and decorative work in a low-trust category is the clearest yes, conditional on the pipeline.
